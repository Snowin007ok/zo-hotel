Part A deliverable

The exit prompt we show when a guest starts to cancel

One cancellation reason, one prompt. Below: the reason I picked and why, the live component, the four required pieces of copy, and the style-guide rule behind each decision.

Reason: dates changed Trigger: “Cancel booking” Pattern: confirmation dialog

The cancellation reason I picked

The dataset behind this assignment holds 119,390 bookings, 37% of which were cancelled. Splitting those bookings by what happened to them before the cancellation gives a clear ranking of where the risk sits. The dataset records booking signals, not reasons the guest wrote down, so what follows is a reading of those patterns rather than a measured cause.

Cancellation rate within each group. Higher means the group cancelled more often.
Signal in the booking Cancellation rate What it suggests the guest felt
Booking never modified 41% Nothing could be adjusted, so the only exit was cancelling
Booking changed once 14% Something could be adjusted, so the trip survived
Non-refundable rate 99% Locked in, and cancelled anyway
No special request attached 48% Nothing attached to the booking, so nothing to lose
Booked more than six months ahead 56% Plans moved in the months between booking and arrival
The reason I chose: “my dates changed.” It is the reason with the cheapest fix and the clearest signal in the data. Bookings changed once were cancelled 14% of the time, against 41% for bookings nobody touched. Long lead times point the same way — 56% of bookings made more than six months ahead were cancelled, which is consistent with plans moving between booking and arrival. These are correlations rather than proof of cause, but that pattern suggests offering date changes may help preserve bookings when plans move. So the prompt does not argue with the guest. It offers different dates.

The four required pieces

Headline

Change your dates instead?

The header carries the verb the whole dialog is built on, so the guest reads the alternative before reading anything about cancelling. Sentence case, no exclamation point, and no “are you sure”. An earlier draft read “Plans changed?”, which named the situation but shared no verb with the button underneath it — the manual asks for one verb across header, description and call to action, so the header now states the action on offer.

Persuasive message

Change your dates and keep your booking. You have one free date change left on your Semi-flex rate. Any price difference is shown before you confirm.

Then the one consequence of not taking it, from their own booking:

Cancel today and ₹3,606 of ₹7,212 is refundable.

Three sentences, each carrying one fact: the action, what it costs in changes, and what it costs in money. The verb matches the button — the description says change your dates and the button says Change my dates. The rate name and the free-change count are read from the guest's own booking, so none of it reads as a template. An earlier draft opened with “You may not need to give up your stay” and then listed the room, the nightly rate, the free changes and the cancellation deadline as bullets, with a closing line about fees. The manual asks for only the information relevant to the decision and no redundancy, so the room rate came out, the fee line folded into the description, and the refund became the single consequence line above.

Call to action

Continue cancellation Change my dates

Two choices, so the decision is legible at a glance. Change my dates is the affirmative action, sits on the right, and names exactly what it does. Nothing here says Yes, No, OK, or Proceed. The route the guest originally asked for stays fully available and readable as Continue cancellation, but it is a quiet text button on the far side of the dialog rather than the default. An earlier draft carried a third button, Keep my booking, between the two: it duplicated what closing the dialog already does, so it came out. The close control, Escape and the backdrop all keep the booking, and the close button says so.

Image

A photograph of the poolside the guest is about to give up, rather than an icon or an illustration. It carries alternative text for screen readers, sits under a light gradient so the close control stays visible, and is never displayed larger than its own pixel dimensions.

Where it appears

A modal dialog, shown the moment a guest chooses Cancel booking on the manage page. Focus lands on Change my dates, Esc and the backdrop both keep the booking, and focus returns to the button the guest came from. It appears once per cancellation attempt, so it never becomes the noise the style guide warns about.

Why the copy is written this way

Each row is a rule from the Zoho UI Best Practices manual and what it changed in this component.

Rule from the manual What it changed here
Default to sentence case Every heading, label, and button on the site is sentence case. The only all-caps text is the small section eyebrow, which the manual allows for demarcating sections.
Periods only for complex or multi-sentence microcopy The headline has none. “Free changes carry no fee, and any price difference is shown before you confirm.” keeps one, because the comma makes it complex.
Use exclamation points sparingly, never in confirmations or CTAs There is not one exclamation point anywhere in this project.
Avoid “Are you sure” language The confirmation step asks “Cancel booking?” and then states the consequence, instead of asking the guest to reassure us.
Use the same action verb in header, description, and CTA The exit prompt uses change in all three: “Change your dates instead?”, “Change your dates and keep your booking…”, and the button “Change my dates”. The confirmation uses cancel in all three: “Cancel booking?”, “Cancel your ZO Hotel Mumbai booking for 14–16 Aug…”, and the button “Cancel booking”. The other two variants do the same with send and confirm.
Never use generic CTAs Buttons read Change my dates, Keep booking, Cancel booking, Continue cancellation, Add requests, Find my booking. No Yes, No, OK, or Proceed.
Put the affirmative CTA on the right, and add friction for critical actions In the exit prompt the affirmative action, Change my dates, is the emphasised button on the right. In the confirmation step the order deliberately flips: the emphasised right-hand button is Keep booking and cancelling is the quiet button on the left. That is the manual's “more friction” pattern, chosen deliberately because cancelling costs the guest money and cannot be undone.
Reserve a forcing function for the most extreme cases Where cancelling returns the guest nothing — a Saver booking, or any booking inside its refund window — the header reads “Cancel non-refundable booking?” and Cancel booking stays unavailable until the guest types CANCEL, with a live region saying why. Keep booking stays available throughout. On refundable rates there is no forcing function, because that friction would be theatre.
Avoid “Click here” and “Learn more” Links name their destination: “manage booking page”, “See the exit prompt built on this”, “See flexible booking options”.
Error messages state the issue and the fix, without blame “Please pick a reason so we know what to fix.” “Check-out must be at least one night after check-in.” Never “You didn't…”.
Apologise only for system errors Sold-out dates get “Sorry, we are fully booked over the new year period.” A mistyped booking reference does not get an apology, just the way forward.
Success alerts are short, with at most one action “Dates changed”, with a single Undo. The pattern is {thing} {action done}.
Never put critical information in a temporary alert The cancellation outcome, refund amount, and refund reference go in a persistent banner that stays until dismissed. Only reversible successes use a toast.

What I deliberately did not write

The version on the left is what this dialog looks like when nobody applies the manual. It is also what most booking sites ship.

Don't

Warning

Are you sure you want to cancel this booking?

Cancel OK
  • Alarmist header on a message that is not an emergency
  • “Are you sure” adds words and no information
  • “Cancel” and “OK” are ambiguous: one cancels the dialog, the other cancels the holiday
  • No consequence stated: no refund figure, no deadline, no mention of the room
  • No alternative offered, so the only exit is the one that loses the booking

Do

Change your dates instead?

Change your dates and keep your booking. You have one free date change left on your Semi-flex rate. Any price difference is shown before you confirm.

Continue cancellation Change my dates
  • One verb — change — in the header, the description and the button
  • Every button says what it does to the booking
  • Two choices, and only the facts that bear on this decision
  • Offers the alternative the data supports, and keeps cancelling one click away

Two more variants, for the next two reasons

The same component answers the other high-cancellation signals in the data. Both are live in the demo above.

Reason: found a lower price

Found it cheaper somewhere else?

Send us the link and you may not need to cancel at all. If the same room and dates are publicly listed for less, we match that price and take another 10% off it.

CTA: Send the price link — which opens a one-field form with URL validation.
Evidence: third-party bookings cancelled 41% of the time, against 17% booked direct.

Reason: requests not confirmed

Waiting on something?

You may not need to give up your stay. Tell us what you need — parking, early check-in, a cot, a Jain meal — and we confirm it in writing before you arrive.

CTA: Confirm my requests — which opens the requests dialog.
Evidence: 48% cancellation with no request attached, 22% with one confirmed.
